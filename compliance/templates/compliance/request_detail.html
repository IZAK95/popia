  1. Verify the person's identityConfirm they are who they say they are (e.g. call back on the number on file, or ask for a copy of ID). Never send information to an unverified requester (s23).
  2. Acknowledge receiptLet them know you received the request and when to expect an answer. Use the response letter templates. Requests may arrive by email, phone, WhatsApp or letter and may not be refused because they're not on the official form.
    3. {% if object.request_type == "access" %}
  3. Find the informationSearch email, files, HR/payroll, accounting and paper files. Confirming whether you hold information about them is free (s23(1)(a)). If you hold none, say so in writing.
  4. Give a fee estimate first, if you chargeIf you'll charge for copies, send a written estimate before doing the work; you may ask for a deposit (s23(3)). Someone asking for their own information pays no request fee.
  5. Decide within 30 daysTell them what you hold, who has had access to it, and give copies (s23(1)(b)). Refuse only on a PAIA ground (e.g. s63 another person's privacy, s64 commercial information of a third party, s67 legal privilege), and disclose every part you don't refuse (s23(5)). Tell them they may ask for corrections (s23(2)). You may extend once by up to 30 days if you tell them in writing why (PAIA s57).
    6. {% elif object.request_type == "correction" or object.request_type == "deletion" %}
  6. Check the requestIs the information inaccurate, irrelevant, excessive, out of date, incomplete, misleading or unlawfully obtained? Check the retention schedule – some records must be kept by law.
  7. Correct / delete, or explainDo it, or give them credible evidence that the information is right. If you can't agree and they ask, attach a note to the record that a correction was requested but not made (s24(2)). Tell anyone you shared the information with, if the change affects decisions about the person (s24(3)).
    8. {% elif object.request_type == "restriction" %}
  8. Check that restriction appliesYou must restrict processing while you check information they say is wrong, when you only still need it as proof, when processing was unlawful and they prefer restriction to deletion, or when they ask to move it to another system (s14(6)).
  9. Restrict itKeep the information but stop using it: mark the file or move it to a restricted folder so staff can't use it. While restricted you may only store it, or use it as proof, with their consent, to protect someone's rights or in the public interest (s14(7)).
  10. Tell them before lifting itBefore you end the restriction, tell the person in writing (s14(8)). Keep this request open until then and use the notice before lifting a restriction.
    11. {% elif object.request_type == "consent" %}
  11. Stop the processing that relied on consentPeople may withdraw consent at any time (s11(2)(b)), e.g. for biometric clocking, marketing or health information. What you did before the withdrawal stays lawful, and processing on another ground (like a contract or a legal duty) can continue.
  12. Update your recordsRecord the withdrawal next to the original consent, remove them from any marketing lists, and offer an alternative where one is needed (e.g. a PIN instead of a fingerprint).
    13. {% elif object.request_type == "objection" or object.request_type == "marketing" %}
  13. Stop the processingAfter a valid objection you may no longer process the information for that purpose (s11(4)). Marketing opt-outs must always be honoured – add them to your do-not-contact list.
    14. {% else %}
  14. InvestigateLook into the concern and involve the Information Officer.
    15. {% endif %}
  15. Respond in writing and closeRecord the outcome and date here. Tell them they may complain to the Information Regulator if unhappy (eServices portal, or POPIAComplaints@inforegulator.org.za / PAIAComplaints@inforegulator.org.za).
